Chapter 6 (Part 1): Entrepreneurship & Feasibility

Importance of Entrepreneurship:

  • Job creation lowers unemployment via small and medium enterprises.
  • Driving innovation introduces new products and technologies.
  • Economic growth contributes to Gross Domestic Product (GDP) expansion.
  • Social problem solving addresses healthcare and energy gaps.
  • Encouraging self-reliance fosters financial independence.

Challenges in SA Entrepreneurship:

  • High unemployment and low disposable income limit market size.
  • Bureaucracy and red tape create administrative delays.
  • Crime and safety concerns increase security operating costs.
  • Limited access to funding makes securing startup capital difficult.
  • Skills gaps result from educational system limitations.
  • Market access difficulties hinder entering larger supply chains.
  • Lack of mentorship leads to poor decision-making.

Definition of an Entrepreneur:

  • An entrepreneur identifies market opportunities and gathers resources.
  • They create and grow a business venture to satisfy needs.
  • They assume risks and earn profits upon success.

Top South African Entrepreneurs:

  • Elon Musk has a net worth of US $22.2 billion and founded PayPal, Tesla, and SpaceX.
  • Christo Wiese is associated with Pepkor and ShopRite.
  • Patrice Motsepe leads Future Mining and African Rainbow Minerals.
  • Bonang Matheba owns Bonang Matheba Entertainment.

Entrepreneur Characteristics:

  • Innovative creation of new ideas.
  • Risk-taker for goal achievement.
  • Self-confident in personal abilities.
  • Hardworking and dedicated.
  • Goal-oriented focus.
  • Leadership skills to guide others.
  • Decision-making ability.
  • Persistent despite setbacks.
  • Adaptable to environmental changes.
  • Good problem solver.
  • Creative thinking.
  • Organized resource management.

Feasibility vs. Viability:

  • Feasibility asks if a project can be done with available resources.
  • Viability asks if a project is sustainable and profitable long-term.

Market Size & Share:

  • Market size is the total potential sales opportunity measured by revenue or volume.
  • Market share is a company's percentage of total market sales.

Research Techniques:

  • Surveys and questionnaires test customer interest.
  • Interviews capture direct feedback.
  • Social media polls track quick engagement.
  • Pre-selling tests customer purchase commitment.
  • Pop-up stalls provide face-to-face feedback.
  • Observing competition checks existing demand.

Costs & Break-Even:

  • Total costs equal fixed costs plus variable costs.
  • Fixed costs remain constant regardless of sales.
  • Variable costs change based on sales activity.
  • Break-even quantity equals fixed costs divided by contribution margin.

Chapter 6 (Part 2): Business Plan & Market Segmentation

Net Profit Calculations:

  • Profit equals total income minus total expenses.
  • Pro forma income statements project future profit using estimated figures.

Importance of a Business Plan:

  • Provides strategic direction and goals.
  • Assists operational decision-making.
  • Secures financing from banks and investors.
  • Identifies risks and opportunities.
  • Improves resource management.
  • Measures performance against benchmarks.
  • Attracts external partners.
  • Enhances stakeholder communication.

Business Plan Elements:

  • Cover page shows ownership and contact details.
  • Table of contents outlines sections.
  • Executive summary provides a concise one-page overview.
  • Management team highlights staff qualifications.
  • Market analysis covers macro and industry environments.
  • Products plan details unique product qualities.
  • Marketing plan outlines target market and the 4 Ps.
  • Operations plan details plant layout or service processes.
  • Financial plan includes establishment costs, budgets, and pro forma statements.
  • Appendices provide supporting legal and professional documents.

Market Segmentation:

  • Demographic segmentation divides markets by age, gender, or income.
  • Geographic segmentation groups customers by location or climate.
  • Psychographic segmentation classifies consumers by lifestyle and values.
  • Behavioral segmentation analyzes purchasing habits and usage rates.

Chapter 7: Management Control

Defining Control:

  • Control identifies operational errors and implements corrective actions.

Importance of Control:

  • Monitors planning, organizing, and leading activities.
  • Evaluates performance against plans.
  • Serves as a vital foundation for all management functions.

Levels of Control:

  • Individual level monitors worker productivity.
  • Group level evaluates team performance.
  • Organizational level assesses company-wide financial and operational goals.

Types of Control:

  • Pre-control focuses on input resources before work begins.
  • Concurrent control monitors real-time transformation processes.
  • Post-control evaluates final output goods and services.

The Control Process:

  • Step 1 establishes performance standards.
  • Step 2 measures actual performance objectively.
  • Step 3 compares performance against standards.
  • Step 4 takes necessary corrective actions.

Performance Standards:

  • Quantity standards track production or sales volume.
  • Cost standards manage budget constraints.
  • Time standards enforce deadlines.
  • Quality standards limit error rates.
  • Behavior standards mandate employee conduct or training.

General Controls & Benefits:

  • Financial controls monitor fiscal performance.
  • Quality controls utilize benchmarking techniques.
  • Balanced scorecards convert strategy into measurable goals.
  • Control benefits include achieving goals, reducing waste, and improving decisions.

Chapter 8 (Leadership Part 1): Styles & Power

Nature of Leadership:

  • Leadership influences and motivates people toward shared goals.
  • True leadership inspires support rather than using threats.

Leadership vs. Management:

  • Leadership focuses on vision, change, and people.
  • Management focuses on execution, structure, and processes.

Leadership Styles:

  • Autocratic leadership makes decisions without employee consultation.
  • Participative leadership involves collaborative decision-making.
  • Free-rein leadership grants workers complete task autonomy.
  • Situational leadership matches style to specific operational contexts.

Types of Power:

  • Coercive power relies on fear and punishment.
  • Reward power controls valuable perks or pay.
  • Legitimate power derives from organizational titles.
  • Referent power stems from personal charisma and respect.
  • Expert power comes from specialized skills and knowledge.

Chapter 9 (Leadership Part 2): Leadership Theories

Trait Theory:

  • Assumes good leaders are born with innate personal traits.
  • Includes traits like honesty, confidence, and empathy.

Behavioral Theory:

  • Studies how successful leader actions differ from unsuccessful ones.
  • Emphasizes perseverance and focusing on key signals.

Newer Approaches:

  • Leadership competencies theory links skill volume to effectiveness.
  • Principle-centered perspective highlights core leadership beliefs.
  • Ethical-leadership perspective promotes moral conduct.
  • Transformational leadership inspires innovation and cultural change.
  • Transactional leadership exchanges rewards for structured performance.
  • Leader-Member-Exchange (LMX) focuses on in-group and out-group dynamics.
  • Servant-leadership involves leading by example through shared work.

Chapter 10: Leading (Motivation)

Definition and Goals:

  • Employee motivation is the daily enthusiasm, energy, promise, and creativity an employee brings to reach business goals.
  • Management goals include finding the right person, retaining employees, ensuring attendance, encouraging high performance, and fostering good citizenship.

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs:

  • Comprises physiological, safety, social, esteem, and self-actualization needs.
  • Management applies this via salaries, job stability, team interactions, recognition, and autonomy.

Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory:

  • Distinguishes between motivating factors (high motivators like achievement and recognition) and hygiene factors (low motivators like pay and working conditions).
  • Hygiene factors must be present to prevent job dissatisfaction, allowing motivators to increase satisfaction.

Other Theories:

  • Vroom's Theory (Carrot and Stick Approach): Employees are motivated by positive rewards (bonuses) or negative consequences (warnings).
  • Adams' Equity Theory (1965): Employees are demotivated if they perceive unfairness when comparing their input-outcome ratio to coworkers.
  • Alderfer's ERG Theory: Groups needs into Existence, Relatedness, and Growth. Unlike Maslow's sequential model, multiple levels of ERG needs can be satisfied simultaneously.
  • McClelland's Human Motivation Theory: States that every person is driven by one of three primary needs: the need for achievement, affiliation, or power.

Chapter 11: Stakeholders

Definition and Aims:

  • Stakeholders have an interest in a company and affect or are affected by its performance.
  • Businesses aim to promote positive stakeholder actions and prevent negative ones.

Stakeholder Categories & Influence:

  • Owners: Provide capital and loan security, evaluate success. Expect ROI. Can close/sell business or dismiss managers.
  • Customers: Buy products and advertise via word of mouth. Expect good prices/quality. Can stop buying or spread negative feedback.
  • Employees: Provide labor/skills. Expect fair pay, job security, work-life balance. Influence via strikes, quiet quitting, theft, and sabotage.
  • Community: Provide local support. Expect funding/venues. Influence via positive word of mouth, boycotts, protests, or looting.
  • Suppliers: Provide goods/services/finance. Expect timely payments and loyalty. Influence by stopping supplies or changing credit arrangements.
  • Government: Municipalities, SARS, SAPS, CCMA. Expect taxes and ethical behavior. Influence by canceling licenses, imposing fines, or load shedding.

Chapter 12: Assets and Risks (Retail Risk Management)

Introduction to Operational Risks:

  • Owning a retail store or e-commerce business comes with built-in operational risks that impact performance.
  • Top risks include shoplifting, employee theft, fraud, inventory shortages, cybersecurity threats, supply chain disruptions, competition, health and safety incidents, natural disasters, and sabotage.

Shrinkage & Prevention:

  • Shrinkage is the reduction of inventory value due to employee or customer theft, POS mistakes, supplier short-deliveries, or counting errors.
  • Store prevention methods: organized store, optimizing layout near exits, security cameras/mirrors, customer service engagement, and RFID tags.
  • Staff should be trained on spotting price-switching scams, handling suspects calmly, and involving security/police.

Legal Capacity & Prosecution in South Africa:

  • Children under 12 cannot be prosecuted for shoplifting.
  • Children aged 12–14 can only be prosecuted if the State proves criminal capacity.
  • Severely mentally incapacitated individuals may not be held criminally responsible.

Other Risks & Management:

  • Data breaches and phishing scams target sensitive data. Natural disasters require property insurance. Customer injuries require general liability insurance. Forced closures happen due to lockdowns or safety violations.
  • Risk Management Process: Identify risks, evaluate techniques, select technique, implementation, and monitor effectiveness.

Chapter 13: Workplace Health and Safety

OHS Act Purpose & Responsibility:

  • The Occupational Health and Safety Act (OHSA), 1993, requires retailers to maintain a safe work environment free of health risks.
  • Safety is the shared responsibility of both employers and employees. Both parties must proactively identify dangers and develop safety control measures.

Hazards, Risks, and Controls:

  • Hazard: anything with the potential to cause harm (e.g., wet floors, exposed wiring).
  • Risk: the likelihood and severity of harm caused by a hazard.
  • Controls: measures implemented to decrease the likelihood or consequences of an unwanted event.

Employer & Employee Duties:

  • Employer: Identify risks, establish/provide protective methods and training, ensure compliance, appoint health and safety reps.
  • Employee: Care for personal health/safety, avoid "playing" at work, obey safety rules, report unsafe conditions, give info to labour inspectors.

Health and Safety Representatives (HSRs):

  • Required when a workplace has more than 20 employees.
  • Shops require at least 1 HSR per 100 employees, while other workplaces require 1 per 50 employees.

Implementation and Stress:

  • Managed by Department of Labour. Inspectors can enter workplaces without notice, take samples, issue fines, or stop processes.
  • Stress symptoms include physical, behavioral, cognitive, and emotional signs. Coping strategies include identifying causes, managing time, setting priorities/boundaries.
