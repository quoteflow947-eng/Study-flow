Retail Business Management

Chapter 6 (Part 1): Entrepreneurship & Feasibility

Importance of Entrepreneurship:

  • Job creation lowers unemployment via small and medium enterprises.
  • Driving innovation introduces new products and technologies.
  • Economic growth contributes to Gross Domestic Product (GDP) expansion.
  • Social problem solving addresses healthcare and energy gaps.
  • Encouraging self-reliance fosters financial independence.

Challenges in SA Entrepreneurship:

  • High unemployment and low disposable income limit market size.
  • Bureaucracy and red tape create administrative delays.
  • Crime and safety concerns increase security operating costs.
  • Limited access to funding makes securing startup capital difficult.
  • Skills gaps result from educational system limitations.
  • Market access difficulties hinder entering larger supply chains.
  • Lack of mentorship leads to poor decision-making.

Definition of an Entrepreneur:

  • An entrepreneur identifies market opportunities and gathers resources.
  • They create and grow a business venture to satisfy needs.
  • They assume risks and earn profits upon success.

Top South African Entrepreneurs:

  • Elon Musk has a net worth of US $22.2 billion and founded PayPal, Tesla, and SpaceX.
  • Christo Wiese is associated with Pepkor and ShopRite.
  • Patrice Motsepe leads Future Mining and African Rainbow Minerals.
  • Bonang Matheba owns Bonang Matheba Entertainment.

Entrepreneur Characteristics:

  • Innovative creation of new ideas, Risk-taker for goal achievement, Self-confident in personal abilities.
  • Hardworking and dedicated, Goal-oriented focus, Leadership skills to guide others.
  • Decision-making ability, Persistent despite setbacks, Adaptable to environmental changes.
  • Good problem solver, Creative thinking, Organized resource management.

Feasibility vs. Viability:

  • Feasibility asks if a project can be done with available resources.
  • Viability asks if a project is sustainable and profitable long-term.

Market Size & Share:

  • Market size is the total potential sales opportunity measured by revenue or volume.
  • Market share is a company's percentage of total market sales.

Research Techniques:

  • Surveys/questionnaires test interest; Interviews capture direct feedback; Social media polls track engagement.
  • Pre-selling tests purchase commitment; Pop-up stalls provide face-to-face feedback; Observing competition checks existing demand.

Costs & Break-Even:

  • Total costs equal fixed costs plus variable costs.
  • Fixed costs remain constant regardless of sales. Variable costs change based on sales activity.
  • Break-even quantity equals fixed costs divided by contribution margin.

Chapter 6 (Part 2): Business Plan & Market Segmentation

Net Profit Calculations:

  • Profit equals total income minus total expenses.
  • Pro forma income statements project future profit using estimated figures.

Importance of a Business Plan:

  • Provides strategic direction and goals; Assists operational decision-making.
  • Secures financing from banks and investors; Identifies risks and opportunities.
  • Improves resource management; Measures performance against benchmarks.
  • Attracts external partners; Enhances stakeholder communication.

Business Plan Elements:

  • Cover page shows ownership/contact details; Table of contents outlines sections.
  • Executive summary provides a concise one-page overview; Management team highlights staff qualifications.
  • Market analysis covers macro and industry environments; Products plan details unique product qualities.
  • Marketing plan outlines target market and the 4 Ps; Operations plan details plant layout or service processes.
  • Financial plan includes establishment costs, budgets, and pro forma statements; Appendices provide supporting documents.

Market Segmentation:

  • Demographic segmentation divides markets by age, gender, or income.
  • Geographic segmentation groups customers by location or climate.
  • Psychographic segmentation classifies consumers by lifestyle and values.
  • Behavioral segmentation analyzes purchasing habits and usage rates.

Chapter 7: Management Control

Defining Control & Importance:

  • Control identifies operational errors and implements corrective actions.
  • Monitors planning, organizing, and leading activities; Evaluates performance against plans.
  • Serves as a vital foundation for all management functions.

Levels & Types of Control:

  • Levels: Individual (worker productivity), Group (team performance), Organizational (company-wide goals).
  • Types: Pre-control (input resources), Concurrent control (real-time processes), Post-control (final output).

The Control Process & Standards:

  • Step 1 establishes standards; Step 2 measures actual performance objectively; Step 3 compares performance against standards; Step 4 takes corrective actions.
  • Standards: Quantity (volume), Cost (budgets), Time (deadlines), Quality (error rates), Behavior (conduct/training).

General Controls & Benefits:

  • Financial controls, Quality controls (benchmarking), Balanced scorecards (convert strategy to goals).
  • Benefits include achieving goals, reducing waste, and improving decisions.

Chapter 8: Leadership (Part 1)

Nature of Leadership vs. Management:

  • Leadership influences and motivates people toward shared goals; focuses on vision, change, and people.
  • True leadership inspires support rather than using threats.
  • Management focuses on execution, structure, and processes.

Leadership Styles:

  • Autocratic: makes decisions without employee consultation.
  • Participative: involves collaborative decision-making.
  • Free-rein: grants workers complete task autonomy.
  • Situational: matches style to specific operational contexts.

Types of Power:

  • Coercive (fear/punishment), Reward (perks/pay), Legitimate (titles), Referent (charisma/respect), Expert (skills/knowledge).

Chapter 9: Leadership (Part 2)

Theories:

  • Trait Theory: Assumes good leaders are born with innate personal traits (honesty, empathy).
  • Behavioral Theory: Studies how successful leader actions differ from unsuccessful ones.
  • Newer Approaches: Leadership competencies theory (skill volume = effectiveness), Principle-centered (core beliefs), Ethical-leadership (moral conduct).
  • Transformational (inspires innovation), Transactional (exchanges rewards for structured performance).
  • Leader-Member-Exchange (LMX): focuses on in-group and out-group dynamics.
  • Servant-leadership: involves leading by example through shared work.

Chapter 10: Leading (Motivation)

Definition and Goals:

  • Employee motivation is the daily enthusiasm, energy, promise, and creativity an employee brings to reach business goals.
  • Management goals include finding the right person, retaining employees, ensuring attendance, encouraging high performance.

Motivation Theories:

  • Maslow's Hierarchy: physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualization.
  • Herzberg's Two-Factor: Motivating factors (achievement) vs. hygiene factors (pay, working conditions). Hygiene prevents dissatisfaction.
  • Vroom's Theory (Carrot & Stick): motivated by positive rewards or negative consequences.
  • Adams' Equity Theory: demotivated if perceiving unfairness compared to coworkers.
  • Alderfer's ERG Theory: Existence, Relatedness, Growth. Multiple levels satisfied simultaneously.
  • McClelland's Human Motivation Theory: driven by need for achievement, affiliation, or power.

Chapter 11: Stakeholders

Definition and Categories:

  • Stakeholders have an interest in a company and affect or are affected by its performance.
  • Categories: Owners, Customers, Employees, Community, Suppliers, and Government.

Stakeholder Expectations & Influence:

  • Owners: Expect ROI/profits. Can close/sell business.
  • Customers: Expect good prices/quality. Can stop buying or spread negative feedback.
  • Employees: Expect fair pay/balance. Can strike, quiet quit, or sabotage.
  • Community: Expect support/funding. Can boycott, protest, or loot.
  • Suppliers: Expect timely payments/fair negotiation. Can stop supplies or halt credit.
  • Government: Expect taxes/lawfulness. Can cancel licenses or implement load shedding (e.g., Eskom).

Chapter 12: Assets and Risks

Operational Risks & Shrinkage:

  • Risks include shoplifting, employee theft, fraud, cyber threats, natural disasters, and sabotage.
  • Shrinkage is the reduction of inventory value due to theft, POS mistakes, or counting errors.

Legal Capacity in SA:

  • Children under 12 cannot be prosecuted for shoplifting. Children 12–14 prosecuted only if State proves criminal capacity.

Risk Management Process:

  • Identify risks, evaluate techniques, select technique, implementation, and monitor effectiveness.

Chapter 13: Workplace Health and Safety

OHS Act & Responsibilities:

  • OHS Act (1993) main purpose is protecting employees from work-related hazards/injuries.
  • Shared responsibility between employers (provide protective methods/training) and employees (obey rules, no playing).

Hazards vs. Risks vs. Controls:

  • Hazard: potential to cause harm. Risk: likelihood/severity of harm. Controls: measures to decrease likelihood.

Health and Safety Representatives (HSRs):

  • Required when workplace has >20 employees. Shops require 1 HSR per 100 employees.
